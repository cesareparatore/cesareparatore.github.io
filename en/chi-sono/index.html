One curiosity, many directions

At fourteen, I took a computer apart to understand how it worked. In the years that followed, I brought the same curiosity to movement, sport, teaching and organisation. These are distinct experiences, developed in different settings. Their connection is not a claim to universal expertise; it is a habit of asking questions, understanding the problem and choosing the right tools.

01 — Understanding technology

In the late 1990s, between a 56k modem and a CRT monitor, I switched on my first computer. Using it was not enough: I wanted to understand what was inside and why it worked. I opened it, took it apart and put it back together. By the age of nineteen, that curiosity had become my first job.

02 — Discovering movement

Later, I began observing a much more complex system: the human body. Running taught me consistency and how to manage effort; football introduced me to sport through both play and teamwork. Founding and managing a team brought me closer to the organisational responsibilities involved in working with people.

03 — Bringing method to practice

Returning to running strengthened my path as both an athlete and a coach. To explore what I observed in practice, I pursued studies in Sport and Exercise Sciences. University gave me tools to understand movement more systematically and connect practical experience with structured knowledge.

04 — Making complexity understandable

During the pandemic, running remained a source of continuity and reflection. My return coincided with entering the school system as a teacher. Teaching reinforced a simple principle: knowing a subject is not enough; you must be able to explain it, listen to questions and check that it has been understood.

05 — Organising sport projects

To explore the relationship between sport, people and organisation, I continued my studies with a master’s degree in Sport Management. It gave further structure to an interest already present in my experience with the team: a sport project needs goals, roles, resources and shared responsibilities.

06 — Returning to the starting point

Programming and web design brought the technological curiosity of my early years back into focus. Writing code and designing digital tools still means breaking a problem into parts, understanding them and building a solution. This interest led to my website and the digital projects I develop.

The common thread is not doing everything or blending different professions together. It is approaching each field with care for its context, recognising what I can do, what needs further investigation and what requires other expertise.

A method before a solution

Every collaboration starts with the request: listening, goals, constraints and expected outcomes. Only then do we define scope, activities and format. This helps avoid generic promises and makes it clear from the outset whether I am the right person to help.

Curiosity with direction

Working across different fields has taught me not to settle for the first answer. I gather relevant information, distinguish between problems and choose tools that fit the request.

Clear goals and boundaries

Before starting, I explain what the service includes, which steps are planned and what falls outside my professional scope. Timing, format and availability are agreed case by case; no two people or projects can be promised identical outcomes.

Five fields, distinct expertise

Sport management, kinesiology, running coaching, education and web design have different purposes and responsibilities. I describe them separately so it is clear which needs each field addresses and when another professional may be more appropriate.
